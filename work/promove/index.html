01 · 1-minute story

Your character copies you.

02 · Context

Artists can build the body. Motion is the hard part.

In Procreate, an artist draws a character with full control over pose, proportion and personality. Making it move is a different job. You go frame by frame, a second of animation is twenty-four drawings, and the rigging tools that could help are built for technical animators, not doodle artists.

And movement is personal. Everyone carries their own body language: how they lean, turn, shrug. That is exactly what redrawing by hand loses, and exactly what a camera can capture.

03 · Final product

Perform, capture, refine, take it back.

pro-move is an add-on that lives inside Procreate, as one Rig tab in the Actions menu. Everything runs on the device, so a drawing never leaves it.

  1. Perform

    Step in front of the camera. Your character copies your joints and your face: blinks, mouth, smile, brows, head tilt.

  2. Capture

    Pose: a three-second countdown, one drawing. Motion: up to ten seconds, keeping the drawings where you move most.

  3. Refine

    Tap a limb to turn or stretch it. Where a joint tears, a local AI repaints only that spot.

  4. Take it back

    PSD layers that Animation Assist plays directly. Layered SVG, sprite sheet, video and an AI pack.

TVPaint's rigging tool does what we needed, but for technical animators on a desktop. We studied how it behaves and translated each idea for an artist with a finger. Its code is not public, so everything we kept is an equivalent of what we could see.

05 · Artist language

Say it the way an artist would.

No physics, no weights. The character is a hierarchy, and settings flow down: set the feel on an arm and everything inside follows, until you override one part. Overrides are marked, and a part's twin can mirror its edits.

06 · One canvas

The canvas is the interface.

The first version split one job, animating your character, across separate places: a capture bar, a sheet of drawings, a layers panel, a rig panel and a preview window. An artist had to hunt for each one.

So we asked a different question: what if every control sat on the drawing, next to the thing it changes? Capture opens from the top left. The take lands in a filmstrip at the bottom. A small pill appears beside the part you tap. A slim strip on the right holds the feel. One rule decided what could be hidden: a control may only go if it has a visible twin somewhere else.

We compared nine directions as grey-box boards. Pick one.

Safe to simplify

  • Where controls live
  • Wording
  • The arrival sheet
  • Long lists of settings

Risky to cut

  • Hands-free gestures: false triggers, small rooms
  • Hold-to-record: the performer stands far from the iPad
  • Hold and pinch: invisible to a new user

Never simplify

  • Marked jumps
  • Undo and redo
  • Turn versus stretch
  • Every body part, reachable
  • The original take, one press away
07 · The interface

From a recording to a drawing you can edit.

Show motion, do not describe it. Feel tiles are small animated waves. Path is a dotted trail where the spacing shows speed. Hold Original to see the raw camera take. Amber seams mark frames that no longer flow. Tips appear once, then get out of the way.

A take becomes drawings

A recording turns into an arrival sheet of drawings picked where the body moves most, with a Few to Many slider. Reordering marks the spots that break the flow and lets the artist add an in-between. TVPaint-style bones, a turn circle and stretch bring editing onto the character.

Every moment between taps

We designed what happens around each action: a beating countdown that sits away from the face, a REC chip and a red canvas edge while recording, a visible landing slot while dragging, and a toast that says what moved.

The final screens

Six taps from start to finish: open the camera, press the shutter, watch the take land, tap a part and drag its handle, apply to every frame or keep it on one, then pick a feel and tap Done.

08 · Motion

How smooth should it feel?

Raw tracking jitters. Smooth it too little and the character looks like a puppet on strings. Too much and it lags like a bad video call. So smoothness became a design material, not a single slider.

  1. Clean the input

    A One Euro filter removes shaking but keeps fast moves sharp.

  2. Primary motion

    Each bone rides a damped spring that eases in and out and never overshoots.

  3. Secondary motion

    The jacket hem and cuffs lag on fast moves and settle. Head, torso and shoes stay rigid.

One rig, many uses

  • A loading animation: a walk cycle already eases in and out, so it can stand in for a spinner.
  • Facial recognition feedback: eyelids, gaze, brows and mouth follow your face, so the product shows it sees you.

Tuned in a fixed order

  • Fix the deformation with springs and cloth off
  • Then add the secondary springs
  • Smoothing can hide a broken joint, so it proves itself last
09 · Problems

What broke, and what we did.

−29%

elbow width at a 90° bend. Blending two rotations loses volume: the neck narrows 8% at a 45° tilt, and the inner knee 50% past 120°.

~1 hr

per AI frame on the processor alone. The laptop's GPU reported Code 43. A fast mode brought it to about 1.5 minutes.

0.35

redraw strength. The first AI step repainted whole frames and drifted off-model, so it now repaints only the joints that break.

  • Fixed test poses (squat, arms up, crossed elbows, head tilts) so every fix is checked against the same extremes.
  • A rig check walks you through six poses, finds where the drawing chops, and saves each repair as a joint fix that fades in live.
  • Crossed arms flattened into the jacket until draw order followed the nearer wrist.

11 · We tested it

Five artists, five out of five.

We put it in front of five Procreate users at the ASU School of Design, live and in real time. It followed their motion, their body language and their emotions, and each of them edited their own take on the canvas. All five finished the task: a 100% success rate.

13 · Reflections

Measure first, then make it move.

A drawing is not a 3D model. Every trick for making it bend came with a visible cost, and the only way to choose was to test the same extreme poses again and again. Smoothness was a design decision as much as a technical one.

Known limits

  • Tested with five artists so far. A larger group would show more body types and styles
  • Fingers and eyes are not separate parts
  • Takes are lost on reload and not in exports yet
  • Procreate has no public plugin interface, so a real extension needs its cooperation or a companion app

Next

  • A longer test with more artists and more kinds of movement
  • A working GPU
  • Side, back and three-quarter views
  • Lottie export for motion tools
  • A real iPad companion app
