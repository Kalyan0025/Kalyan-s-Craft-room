01 · Context

Maintenance was either too late or too early.

Maintenance models comparisonDocumented

Siemens plants run motors, pumps, turbines, compressors and heat exchangers. Traditionally, they were maintained in one of two ways, and both cost money: waiting for a failure means unplanned downtime, and replacing parts on a calendar means replacing healthy parts.

PMIP was built to move maintenance to a third model, prediction. The intelligence already existed. The step that didn't work yet was the one in the middle: an operator validating the prediction. That is where my work sat.

Model 1

Reactive

  1. Equipment fails
  2. Production is affected
  3. Team investigates
  4. Repair happens

Cost: unplanned downtime.

Model 2

Scheduled preventive

  1. A fixed interval is reached
  2. Inspection or replacement happens
  3. Whether the part needs it or not

Cost: healthy parts replaced early, crews sent unnecessarily.

Model 3 · PMIP

Predictive

  1. Equipment sensors
  2. Operational data
  3. Anomaly detection
  4. Failure prediction
  5. Operator validation · my focus
  6. Preventive maintenance

Goal: fix what is about to fail, before it fails.

Problem reframeDocumented

I stopped asking how to display predictions.

The model might be accurate, but if an experienced engineer doesn't trust it enough to act, its value is limited. I called this the trust problem, and it changed the question the team was answering.

From

How do we display AI predictions?

→
To

How might we help experienced industrial operators understand the severity, evidence, reasoning and appropriate action behind an AI-generated prediction, without replacing their professional judgment?

07 · Patterns over time

Engineers don't think in snapshots. They think in trajectories.

Equipment health timelineDocumented

Research showed a single abnormal reading wasn't enough for a senior engineer. They wanted to know how the asset got here. So instead of only today's value, I designed a longitudinal view that lets them reconcile the AI's prediction with their own knowledge of the machine.

  1. Week 1Normal operation
  2. Week 2Minor vibration deviation
  3. Week 3Repeated threshold violations
  4. Week 4Temperature begins increasing
  5. CurrentPersistent vibration + temperature anomaly
  6. PredictionBearing degradation · High risk
Did this suddenly spike?
Has it gradually deteriorated?
Did anything change after the last maintenance?
Does this match what I know about this machine?

09 · Human + AI

The model recommends. The engineer decides.

Human–AI authority modelDocumented

This is high-stakes industrial software, so I didn't want the interface to imply "AI decides, human follows". The model detects and explains. Authority then passes to the engineer, who evaluates, decides and acts. Select a step to see it on Motor M-204.

Decision flow · agree / disagreeDocumented

Both answers are first-class. Both are useful.

Agreeing moves straight into maintenance without retyping anything. Disagreeing needs a reason, and that reason is information: if experienced engineers keep disagreeing with one type of prediction, the product and ML teams need to know. Choose an answer to follow its path.

      LearningsDocumented

      What I took away.

      Accuracy doesn't create trust.

      A strong model can still be a weak product if people don't understand its outputs. Trust has to be designed.

      Explainability is evidence, not volume.

      The information has to answer the user's decision questions, in the order they ask them.

      Expert UX isn't simplification.

      Experts need depth. The designer's job is hierarchy, context, progressive disclosure and control.

      Human-in-the-loop has to mean something.

      Not "AI says X, user clicks confirm". The engineer evaluates and decides, and that decision teaches the system.
