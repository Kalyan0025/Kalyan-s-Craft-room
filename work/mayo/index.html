Mayo Clinic · AI-native clinical product · Smart Hematology Triage

Nurses weren't short on judgment. They were short on time to use it.

Smart Hematology Triage is an AI-assisted workspace that brings fragmented patient data into one view. The AI recommends and explains; the clinician decides. I drove the design end to end, from research with Mayo clinicians to validated prototype.

01 · The problem

Time was the bottleneck, not the decision.

Fragmentation model

The U.S. loses an estimated $265 billion a year to administrative complexity alone (Shrank et al., JAMA, 2019). In hematology triage, where a delay can postpone a cancer diagnosis by weeks, nurses lose their most valuable minutes before clinical judgment even begins.

07 · First version & testing

I put v1 in front of clinicians and let the friction rewrite it.

Formative usability round

The first design organised triage signals into one view: dashboard, triage overview, decision flow, timeline, EHR and clinical notes. It showed only a technical "Algorithm Performance" readout, with no confidence layer. Then I tested it.

5physicians at Mayo Clinic
1ML researcher
3methods: observed usage, think-aloud walkthroughs, post-test interviews
Finding 01 · TrustTrust hesitation

Even with a labelled AI summary, clinicians re-checked the record before committing, and clarity dropped when they couldn't see why the AI concluded what it did.

→ led to the confidence layer
Finding 02 · WayfindingDecision spread across sections

Triage meant moving between sections to assemble the decision, and documentation sat at the very end of the flow.

→ led to the 4-step decision flow
Finding 03 · ClarityToo dense on first view

The full overview oriented experienced users but overwhelmed first-timers, who needed a simpler entry point.

→ led to depth on demand
Finding 04 · FamiliarityFamiliar visuals oriented faster

Charts clinicians already use reduced the learning curve, so I mirrored them instead of inventing new visualizations.

→ led to the decision flow in chart form

09 · The confidence layer

A single "95%" asks for blind trust. So I made it take apart.

Decision breakdown

This is the part I'm proudest of. I designed confidence to be explainable by construction: every point traces back to a real clinical signal. The weights weren't invented in design. I shaped them with the Mayo ML developer and a hematology subject-matter expert, grounded in real patient data and diagnostic thresholds.

Step through the signals to see how a recommendation earns its 95%.

0%Model confidence
Urgent malignant hematology referral · 95%. The remaining 5% is stated, not hidden: rare differentials and the confirmatory bone-marrow biopsy (flow cytometry, cytogenetics) needed for a definitive diagnosis.
Why it's built this way
Explainable by construction.

The score is assembled from signals already visible in the chart, so "why 95%?" is answerable at a glance.

Calibrated, not binary.

Blast % at +40 outweighs trajectory at +10, so the number moves the way clinical certainty moves. A 70% reads differently from a 95%.

The 5%, accounted for.

The gap names what would close it: a bone-marrow biopsy. Uncertainty that names its remedy earns trust.

One source of truth.

The glance pill and the full breakdown are the same number at different zoom. Nothing to reconcile.

    14 · Accessibility

    Colour-safe, because colour carries clinical risk.

    Vision modes

    When colour encodes urgency, colour-blind-safe design isn't cosmetic. Red-green deficiency affects about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women. Status never depends on colour alone (WCAG AA, Use of Color): every state pairs colour with icon, shape and label, and users can switch to a mode tuned to their vision. Try them.

    The modes live in Settings, alongside the standards the system follows: colour plus icon, AA contrast, visible focus states and screen-reader labels.

    16 · Reflection

    Clinical tools should reduce friction, not add complexity.

    Learnings + next directions

    I connected two research streams, the product decisions and the feasibility conversations with the AI researcher, the ML developer and the Mayo subject-matter expert. Watching physicians and nurses work showed me that human-in-the-loop AI only earns its place when it saves time and keeps clinicians accountable and in control.

    The strongest decision wasn't a screen. It was keeping one shared representation across the model's training, its output and the interface, so trust had somewhere to stand. Hematology was the stepping stone: the goal was a triage system that could extend to every specialty.
